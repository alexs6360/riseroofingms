What Goes Into a Complete Roof

Two estimates for the same house can differ by thousands of dollars, and the number on the front page rarely explains why. The difference is usually in what each one includes.

A roof is a system. Shingles are the part you can see, but they are one of nine components, and the other eight are what decide whether a roof reaches the end of its life or starts failing early at the edges and the joints. Here is what a complete system looks like, from the deck up.

Select a layer to read what it does.

Layer 1 of 9

Roof deck

The structure underneath everything

The flat panels nailed across your rafters — plywood, or OSB, which is the pressed wood-strand equivalent. Every other part of the roof fastens to it, so it has to be sound before anything goes on top. Panels that have gone soft from an old leak need replacing, not covering: new shingles nailed into soft decking have nothing firm to hold on to.

Ice and water shield
In the valleys, and around anything that pokes throughA sticky rubberized membrane that bonds directly to the deck and seals around the nails driven through it. It goes where water concentrates and lingers: the valleys where two roof planes meet, and the edges. Valleys carry the runoff from two slopes at once, which is why they are usually the first place a roof starts leaking.
Drip edge
Along the bottom edge and up the sloped sidesA bent metal strip at the roof's edges that throws runoff clear of the wood and into the gutter. Without it, water clings to the underside of the shingle and wicks back onto the fascia — the board the gutter hangs on — which rots quietly out of sight until the gutter starts pulling away.
Synthetic underlayment
Across the whole deckA tough woven sheet rolled over the entire deck before the shingles go on. It is the backup layer: when wind drives rain sideways under a shingle, this is what the water lands on instead of your decking. It also keeps the deck dry during the days the roof is open.
Starter strip
The first row of shingles, along the bottom edgeA purpose-made strip with a factory adhesive line that runs along the very bottom edge. The first row of shingles seals down onto it. Without a proper starter the bottom edge has nothing to bond to, and the eave is exactly where wind gets under a roof and begins peeling it upward.
Flashing
Chimneys, walls, vents and skylightsShaped metal at every point where the roof meets something that is not roof. These junctions are where most leaks actually start — not out in the open stretches of shingle. Flashing carried over from the old roof has already spent twenty years bending in the heat and corroding, and it ends up buried under the new shingles where you cannot inspect it.
Architectural shingles
The surface you can seeThe visible layer. Architectural shingles are built from two bonded layers, which makes them heavier and thicker than the flat three-tab kind and gives the roof its depth and shadow line. The weight is also what helps them stay put in wind.
Ridge vent
Along the peakA continuous vent running the length of the peak that lets hot, damp air escape the attic. It only works if air can get in low down under the eaves, so the two are designed as a pair. Trap that heat and moisture instead and it cooks the shingles from underneath and condenses on the framing through winter.
Ridge cap shingles
Over the peak, covering the ventThicker shingles made specifically to fold over the peak and cover the vent's opening. They are pre-shaped for that bend. Ordinary shingles from the main surface, cut down to size, will do the job for a while, but they were never meant to fold, and they crack along the fold line years earlier.

Not sure what's on your roof?

Most of this is invisible from the ground, and some of it stays invisible even from the roof once the shingles are down. An inspection tells you what you actually have. It's free, takes 30 to 45 minutes, and you get photographs and a written estimate at the end.
