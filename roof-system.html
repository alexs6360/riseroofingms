What Goes Into a Complete Roof

Two estimates for the same house can differ by thousands of dollars, and the number on the front page rarely explains why. The difference is usually in what each one includes.

A roof is a system. Shingles are the part you can see, but they sit on top of everything else, and it is what goes underneath that decides whether a roof reaches the end of its life or starts failing early at the edges and the joints. Here is what a complete system looks like, from the deck up.

Select a layer, or pick one from the list.

  1. The structure underneath everything

    The flat panels nailed across your rafters — plywood, or OSB, which is the pressed wood-strand equivalent. Every other part of the roof fastens to it, so it has to be sound before anything goes on top. Panels that have gone soft from an old leak need replacing, not covering: new shingles nailed into soft decking have nothing firm to hold on to.

  2. In the valleys

    A sticky rubberized membrane that bonds directly to the deck and seals around the nails driven through it. It goes in the valleys, where two roof planes meet and the runoff from both slopes converges into one channel. That is more water than anywhere else on the roof has to carry, which is why valleys are usually the first place a roof starts leaking.

  3. Across the whole deck

    A tough woven sheet rolled over the entire deck before the shingles go on. It is the backup layer: when wind drives rain sideways under a shingle, this is what the water lands on instead of your decking. It also keeps the deck dry during the days the roof is open.

  4. The first strip down, along the bottom edge and up the sloped sides

    A purpose-made strip with a factory adhesive line that runs along the eaves and the rakes — the bottom edge of the roof and its sloped sides. The first row of shingles seals down onto it, and so does the outer edge of every row up the sides. Without a proper starter those edges have nothing to bond to, and both are exactly where wind gets under a roof and begins peeling it upward.

  5. The surface you can see

    The visible layer. Architectural shingles are built from two bonded layers, which makes them heavier and thicker than the flat three-tab kind and gives the roof its depth and shadow line. The weight is also what helps them stay put in wind.

  6. Along the peak

    A continuous vent running the length of the peak that lets hot, damp air escape the attic. It only works if air can get in low down under the eaves, so the two are designed as a pair. Trap that heat and moisture instead and it cooks the shingles from underneath and condenses on the framing through winter.

  7. Over the peak, covering the vent

    Thicker shingles made specifically to fold over the peak and cover the vent's opening. They are pre-shaped for that bend. Ordinary shingles from the main surface, cut down to size, will do the job for a while, but they were never meant to fold, and they crack along the fold line years earlier.

  8. Along the bottom edge and up the sloped sides

    A bent metal strip at the roof's edges that throws runoff clear of the wood and into the gutter. Without it, water clings to the underside of the shingle and wicks back onto the fascia — the board the gutter hangs on — which rots quietly out of sight until the gutter starts pulling away.

  9. Chimneys, walls, vents and skylights

    Shaped metal at every point where the roof meets something that is not roof. These junctions are where most leaks actually start — not out in the open stretches of shingle. Flashing carried over from the old roof has already spent twenty years bending in the heat and corroding, and it ends up buried under the new shingles where you cannot inspect it.

Not sure what's on your roof?

Most of this is invisible from the ground, and some of it stays invisible even from the roof once the shingles are down. An inspection tells you what you actually have. It's free, takes 30 to 45 minutes, and you get photographs and a written estimate at the end.
